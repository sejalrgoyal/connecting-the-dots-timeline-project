Gender and Slavery
in Colonial America

How colonial laws transformed slavery into a hereditary system through gender and reproduction.

This exhibit explores how gender shaped the legal development of slavery in colonial America. Through three pivotal events spanning more than a century, we trace how colonial legislatures tied enslaved status to the mother, and how individuals resisted those structures even as the law tightened its hold.

About This Exhibit

Project Overview


This digital history exhibit was created as a "Connecting the Dots" midterm project for HIST 2384.001. Through primary source analysis and historical argumentation, it examines how gender became the central mechanism through which slavery was legally defined, enforced, and challenged in colonial America.

The exhibit traces three pivotal moments spanning 1656 to 1773, showing how colonial legislators deliberately shifted from English common law principles to statutes that made slavery fully hereditary through the maternal line. The primary sources examined include a Virginia court committee report, a colonial legislative act, and a 19th-century newspaper account of a freedom claim.

Exhibit Thesis

This exhibit argues that between 1656 and 1773, colonial Virginia law transformed slavery from a flexible status shaped partly by paternal lineage into a rigid, hereditary system anchored in enslaved women's bodies. At the same time, the lives and actions of Elizabeth Key and Ona Judge show that enslaved women used both the courts and flight to contest that gendered legal order. Taken together, these moments reveal how gender, law, and slavery developed together across more than a century.

Central Argument

Gender shaped both the legal construction of slavery and the forms of resistance to it. By tying enslaved status to the mother, colonial lawmakers made reproduction itself an instrument of bondage, yet individuals like Ona Judge demonstrated that no law could fully extinguish personal agency.

Historical Context
Colonial America through the Early United States

Setting the Stage


Slavery in the colonial period and early United States was becoming legally and socially institutionalized, with lawmakers crafting systems that enforced racial hierarchies and hereditary bondage. As part of this process, colonial legislatures increasingly linked slavery to gender and reproduction, creating laws that ensured enslaved women's children would also be enslaved.

This legal strategy allowed slavery to expand across generations and sustain the labor system that the colonies, and later the United States, depended on.

Yet even as legal structures tightened control and restricted freedom, resistance persisted. Individuals like Elizabeth Key and Ona Judge demonstrated that these laws, while powerful, could not fully extinguish personal agency.

Bringing these three markers together highlights a long arc of legal change: from a moment when English common law still allowed enslaved people to claim freedom through a free father, to the statute that closed that opening, to the lived reality of a woman born into that hardened system who nevertheless asserted her freedom.

* * *
Thematic Focus

Gender and Slavery


Gender shaped legal definitions and experiences of slavery, particularly through women's reproductive roles. Colonial laws increasingly tied children's status to their mothers, making women's bodies central to the reproduction of slavery.

This connection persisted over time, influencing the lives of enslaved women and their children across generations.

1656
Elizabeth Key Wins Her and Her Son's Freedom
Court Case -- Virginia Colony

Elizabeth Key, the daughter of a free English father and an enslaved African mother, sued for her freedom in Virginia. The court ruled in her favor based on English common law, which stated that a child's status could follow the father.

Primary Source

"Recorded That by the Comon Law the Child of a Woman slave begott by a freeman ought to bee free."

A Report of a Comittee from an Assembly Concerning the freedome of Elizabeth Key (1656)

How this evidence supports the argument: The committee's wording shows that, before 1662, English common law could still recognize freedom through a free father, revealing a moment when hereditary slavery through the mother had not yet been fully codified.

Historical Significance

Her case illustrates an early moment of legal flexibility, showing that in the mid-1600s slavery had not yet become fully hereditary through maternal status.

Connection to gender and slavery: Key's lawsuit centers on the clash between her mother's enslaved status and her father's freedom, illustrating how legal debates over slavery were already organized around women's reproductive status and the question of whether children would inherit bondage.

1662
The Virginia Capitol -- seat of the colonial legislature that passed the 1662 Slave Act, binding children's status to their mothers.
Enlarge

Historical illustration: Virginia, 1662 -- A colonial legislative act codifying hereditary slavery through the mother's condition.

1662
Virginia Slave Act
Legislation -- Virginia General Assembly

The Virginia Slave Act established that a child's status would follow the condition of the mother, meaning children born to enslaved women would automatically be enslaved, closing the legal loopholes that allowed cases like Elizabeth Key's.

Primary Source

"All children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother."

Virginia Slave Act (1662)

How this evidence supports the argument: The statute's language explicitly ties enslaved status to the mother's condition, providing direct proof that lawmakers deliberately turned enslaved women's reproductive capacity into the legal engine of hereditary slavery.

Historical Significance

This law made slavery fully hereditary through the maternal line, ensuring that slavery would be passed down through enslaved women across generations.

Connection to gender and slavery: By making children's freedom or bondage depend entirely on their mothers, the 1662 act placed enslaved women's bodies at the center of colonial labor policy, revealing how gendered ideas about reproduction underwrote the expansion of racial slavery.

1773
Mount Vernon, Virginia -- George Washington's estate, the household from which Ona Judge escaped to claim her freedom.
Enlarge

Historical illustration: Colonial America, 1773 -- Ona Judge escaping bondage under a starlit sky. The mansion at left represents Mount Vernon.

1773
Ona Judge Is Born
Personal History -- Mount Vernon, Virginia

Ona Judge was born enslaved to Martha Washington. She later escaped from George Washington's household in 1796 and successfully lived as a free woman, asserting her freedom despite the laws designed to keep her enslaved.

Primary Source

"No, I am free."

Thomas H. Archibald, "Washington's Runaway Slave," Granite Freeman (Concord, NH), May 22, 1845

How this evidence supports the argument: Judge's refusal to return, captured in the phrase "No, I am free," shows how an enslaved woman born into a fully hereditary, gendered system of slavery still claimed freedom on her own terms, challenging the legal logic built across the previous events.

Historical Significance

Ona Judge's declaration shows her determination to remain free despite laws designed to control enslaved women and their children. Her escape demonstrates that personal agency persisted even under the harshest legal constraints.

Connection to gender and slavery: Judge's life reveals how a woman born under matrilineal slavery navigated, and ultimately rejected, the expectations placed on enslaved women's labor and loyalty within elite households, highlighting gendered forms of resistance to a gendered system of bondage.

Argument

Change and Continuity


Change

A transition occurred from English common law, under which children of free fathers could claim freedom, to colonial laws that tied slavery exclusively to the mother's status. This shift made slavery fully hereditary and closed earlier legal pathways to freedom.

Continuity

Enslaved women and their children remained under systemic control throughout this period, and gender continued to shape both the forms of oppression they endured and the ways they resisted that oppression.

Supporting Evidence

Elizabeth Key, the Virginia Slave Act, and Ona Judge together illustrate the evolving role of gender in defining, sustaining, and challenging slavery. Their stories span more than a century, tracing how legal systems changed while the experience of enslaved women and the drive toward freedom persisted.

Connecting the Events

A Thread Across Generations


1
1656

Elizabeth Key and the Loophole

Elizabeth Key's successful freedom lawsuit exposed early legal loopholes where paternity could determine a child's status. Her victory under English common law revealed that slavery had not yet been made permanently hereditary, and that colonial officials were still deciding how far they were willing to depart from English precedent to meet local labor demands.

2
1662

The Virginia Slave Act Closes the Door

In response to cases like Key's, the Virginia Slave Act of 1662 redefined slavery as inheritable through the mother, ensuring that children born to enslaved women would remain enslaved regardless of their father's status. This statute converted women's reproductive labor into a legal mechanism for reproducing slavery itself, tightening the link between gender, race, and property.

3
1773

Ona Judge and Persistent Resistance

More than a century later, Ona Judge was born into this hardened system of hereditary slavery. Her eventual escape from the Washington household, and her refusal to return even when approached by presidential representatives, demonstrates that enslaved women continued to interpret, negotiate, and defy the legal order that claimed them as inheritable property.

Why These Moments Go Together

Viewed together, these three markers trace the deliberate legal shift from a system in which some enslaved people could still claim freedom through a free father, to a statute that fixed enslaved status through the mother, to the lived experience of a woman born into that system who refused to accept its terms. Each moment illuminates a different stage in the co-production of gender, law, and slavery in colonial and early national America.

Elizabeth Key's case shows lawmakers confronting the tension between English common law and colonial practice. The 1662 act shows them resolving that tension by writing a new, gendered rule into statute. Ona Judge's story shows how, even after that rule had shaped generations of enslaved families, enslaved women continued to exercise agency by claiming freedom in defiance of the law.

Together, these events show how gender shaped both the enforcement of slavery and the ways enslaved people contested it across generations. The arc from Key to the Virginia Act to Judge is not simply a story of increasing restriction; it is also a story of enduring resistance that survived each tightening of the law.

* * *
Conclusion

Gender, Law, and the Limits of Control


The legal transformation examined in this exhibit reveals how gender became a foundational instrument in the construction of American slavery. By anchoring enslaved status to the condition of the mother, colonial lawmakers ensured that slavery would reproduce itself across generations, embedding racial hierarchy into the very act of birth.

Elizabeth Key's case shows us a moment before that system was fully sealed. The Virginia Slave Act of 1662 demonstrates the deliberate legal work required to close the openings her victory exposed. And Ona Judge's life reminds us that no legal structure, however thorough, could entirely suppress the human drive toward freedom.

Together, these three moments illustrate both the power and the limits of law. Slavery was not a natural condition; it was constructed, revised, and enforced through deliberate legislative choices. Understanding those choices, and understanding the resistance they provoked, is essential to understanding the history of the United States itself.

Limits of the Sources

The primary sources used here were produced within unequal power structures: court records and statutes written by colonial officials, and a newspaper account that frames Ona Judge through an antislavery editor's voice. They rarely preserve enslaved women's words in full, and they omit the perspectives of countless others who lived under the same laws. Reading these documents critically—alongside lecture materials and scholarship—helps us see both what they reveal about gendered slavery and what remains obscured or unsaid.

The dots connect across more than a century: from a courtroom in colonial Virginia, to a legislative act that reshaped the lives of generations, to a woman who walked away from the household of the first president and refused to return. Each event illuminates the others. Taken together, they tell a story that is still being reckoned with.

Key Terms

Glossary of Concepts


These brief definitions clarify how this exhibit uses core historical and legal terms. They are meant to make the argument transparent and to show how concepts from lecture and readings help interpret the primary sources.

Hereditary Slavery / Partus Sequitur Ventrem

A legal principle stating that a child's status follows that of the mother. In colonial Virginia, it meant that children born to enslaved women were automatically enslaved, making women's reproduction central to the continuation of slavery.

English Common Law

The body of legal customs and precedents inherited from England. Before colonial statutes reshaped it, common law often assumed that a child's status could follow the father, which made cases like Elizabeth Key's possible.

Gendered Law

Legal rules that assign different rights, obligations, or statuses based on gender. In this exhibit, it refers to laws that used women's reproductive roles to define and reproduce slavery across generations.

Agency

The capacity of individuals to make choices and act within, against, or around systems of power. Elizabeth Key's lawsuit and Ona Judge's escape are examples of enslaved women's agency within a deeply unequal legal order.

Primary Source

A document or artifact produced during the time period being studied. The committee report on Elizabeth Key, the 1662 Virginia statute, and the 1845 newspaper account of Ona Judge are all primary sources used to build this exhibit's argument.

Change and Continuity

A historical method for analyzing what transforms over time and what remains stable. Here, it frames how legal rules about gender and slavery changed between 1656 and 1773 while the basic project of controlling enslaved women and their children persisted.
